# Why Restaurants Are Conflict Hot Spots

Restaurants put a lot of people in a small, hot, loud space under constant time pressure. Everyone depends on everyone else, and money depends on the whole team performing. It's no surprise tempers flare.

## Common sources of conflict

| Source | What it looks like |
| Pressure and speed | Snapping at each other during a rush |
| FOH vs. BOH tension | Servers blaming the kitchen for slow food; cooks blaming servers for wrong tickets |
| Uneven workloads | Some people doing more side work, getting more tables, or covering more shifts |
| Money | Tip pools, tip-outs, and section assignments that feel unfair |
| Scheduling | Favoritism, last-minute changes, shift swaps gone wrong |
| Personalities and gossip | Cliques, rumors, and personal drama spilling into work |
| Poor communication | Unclear expectations, assumptions, and things "lost in translation" |

Conflict isn't always bad. Handled well, it can fix real problems, like an unfair side work system or a broken ticket process. Handled badly, it poisons the whole team.

# Understanding Communication Styles

Most people lean toward one of four styles. Knowing yours, and recognizing others', makes conflict easier to navigate.

| Style | What it looks like | The effect |
| Passive | Staying quiet, avoiding conflict, going along to keep the peace | Resentment builds; problems don't get fixed |
| Aggressive | Yelling, blaming, interrupting, intimidating | People get defensive or shut down |
| Passive-aggressive | Sarcasm, silent treatment, gossip, "forgetting" on purpose | Trust breaks down; tension lingers |
| Assertive | Stating needs clearly and respectfully while listening to others | Problems get solved; respect grows |

## The goal: assertive communication

Assertive communication means being honest about what you need while respecting the other person. It sounds like:

- "I need..." instead of "You never..."
- "I noticed..." instead of "You always..."
- "Can we figure out..." instead of "This is your fault."

## The "I" statement formula

**When** [specific situation], **I feel** [emotion], **because** [impact]. **Could we** [request]?

Example: "When my tickets get fired late, I feel stressed, because my tables wait and my tips drop. Could we go over the timing process together before tomorrow's shift?"

# Communicating Under Pressure

The middle of a rush is the worst time for a deep conversation, but the most common time for conflict.

## During the rush

- **Keep it short and specific.** "I need table 12's salmon re-fired, no butter, allergy."
- **Stay neutral.** Tone matters more than words when everyone's stressed.
- **Save the bigger conversation** for later: "Can we talk about this after the rush?"
- **Don't take the heat personally.** Someone snapping in the weeds often isn't about you.

## Take a pause before responding

When you feel your temper rising, try **STOP**:

- **Stop** what you're about to say.
- **Take** a breath, or two.
- **Observe** what's actually happening and how you're feeling.
- **Proceed** with a calm, intentional response.

> If you need to, step away for 30 seconds: check the walk-in, refill ice, or take a quick breather. It's better than saying something you'll regret.

# FOH and BOH: Working with the Kitchen

The front and back of house often see the same problem from opposite sides. Building a good relationship with the kitchen makes every shift easier.

## What the kitchen wishes servers knew

- A ticket with missing modifiers or allergies creates problems that are hard to fix mid-rush.
- "Where's my food?" every two minutes slows them down.
- Ringing in orders all at once creates bottlenecks.
- A "thank you" at the end of the night goes a long way.

## What servers wish the kitchen knew

- Guests blame the server for slow or wrong food, and it hits our tips.
- We're often relaying guest requests we don't control.
- A quick heads-up about 86'd items or delays helps us manage guests.

## Building a better relationship

- Learn the cooks' names and treat them with respect.
- Ask questions about dishes when it's slow, not during a rush.
- Own your mistakes: "That was my ticket error, sorry. Can you re-fire it?"
- Share positive guest feedback with the kitchen.

# Conflict with Coworkers

## Before you address it, ask yourself

- Is this a one-time thing or a pattern?
- Could there be a misunderstanding?
- What outcome do I actually want?
- Is this something I can handle directly, or do I need a manager?

## The 5-step conversation

1. **Pick the right time and place:** private, calm, not during service.
2. **Open with respect:** "Hey, do you have a few minutes to talk about something?"
3. **Describe, don't accuse:** use "I" statements and specific examples.
4. **Listen to their side** without interrupting. Ask questions.
5. **Agree on a solution** you can both live with, and follow up.

## Scripts for common situations

| Situation | What you can say |
| A coworker skips side work | "I've noticed the side work list hasn't been done on our shared closes. Can we split it up so it's fair?" |
| Someone steals your tables | "I think table 9 was mine per the seating chart. Can we check with the host so we're on the same page?" |
| A coworker is rude to you | "I didn't love how that came across earlier. I'd rather we talk to each other with respect, even when it's busy." |
| Someone gossips about you | "I heard some things were said about me. I'd rather you come to me directly if there's a problem." |
| A shift swap falls through | "We agreed on the swap for Saturday, and it didn't happen. Can we confirm swaps in writing going forward?" |
| Tip-out disagreements | "Can we look at the tip-out sheet together? I want to make sure I understand how it was calculated." |

! If a coworker's behavior involves harassment, threats, discrimination, or violence, don't try to handle it alone. Report it to a manager or HR, and document it (see Guide 03).

# Conflict with Management

Disagreeing with a manager can feel risky. Done well, it can build respect and solve real problems.

## Tips for talking to a manager

- **Ask for a private meeting** at a calm time: "Do you have 10 minutes before the shift?"
- **Come prepared** with specifics: dates, examples, and a suggested solution.
- **Focus on the business impact:** "When I'm triple-sat without a busser, service slows and guests complain."
- **Stay professional,** even if they don't.
- **Follow up in writing:** "Thanks for meeting today. Just to recap, we agreed that..."

## Scripts for common manager conversations

| Situation | What you can say |
| Unfair scheduling | "I've noticed my shifts dropped over the past three weeks. Can you help me understand why, and what I can do to get more hours?" |
| Being corrected in front of guests | "I want to fix mistakes, and I'm open to feedback. Could we have those conversations privately?" |
| Unclear expectations | "I want to make sure I'm meeting your standards. Could you walk me through what you're looking for on closes?" |
| Asking for help during a rush | "I'm at capacity and my tables are waiting. Can you help run food or seat the next party elsewhere?" |
| Receiving a write-up you disagree with | "I'd like to add my written response to this before I sign it." |

## When a conversation isn't enough

If a manager ignores your concerns, retaliates, or treats you unfairly, go up the chain: a general manager, owner, or HR. Document everything, and see Guides 03 and 06 for next steps.

# De-escalation: Calming a Heated Moment

When someone is already angry, whether it's a coworker, a manager, or a guest, your goal is to lower the temperature.

## Do

- Keep your voice low and slow.
- Keep your body relaxed and non-threatening. Don't cross your arms or step closer.
- Acknowledge the emotion: "I can tell you're frustrated."
- Offer a next step: "Let's figure this out after the rush."

## Don't

- Match their volume or tone.
- Say "Calm down." (It almost never works.)
- Argue about who's right in the moment.
- Bring up past issues.

! If anyone becomes physically threatening, remove yourself from the situation and get a manager or security immediately. Your safety comes first.

# Receiving and Giving Feedback

## Receiving feedback

- Listen without interrupting, even if you disagree.
- Ask clarifying questions: "Can you give me an example?"
- Thank the person, then take time to think it over.
- If feedback feels unfair, respond calmly later, with specifics.

## Giving feedback (as a coworker, trainer, or lead)

- Give it privately, and promptly.
- Be specific about the behavior, not the person.
- Include what's going well, too.
- Offer help: "Want me to show you how I do the closing checklist?"
