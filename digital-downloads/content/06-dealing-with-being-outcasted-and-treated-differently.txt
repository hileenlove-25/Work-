# You're Not Imagining It

Being left out, singled out, or held to a different standard at work is painful. It can make you second-guess yourself, dread your shifts, and feel invisible to the people who control your schedule and income.

Many people in this position spend months wondering: *Is it me? Am I being too sensitive? Is this really happening?* This guide will help you look at what's happening clearly, protect yourself, and choose a path forward.

## What being outcasted can look like

- You're scheduled for fewer or worse shifts than coworkers with similar experience.
- You get the slow or undesirable sections again and again.
- Rules are strictly enforced for you but not for others.
- You're left out of group chats, meetings, pre-shift information, or team events.
- Your manager is friendly with others but cold, short, or dismissive with you.
- You're criticized in public while others are corrected in private, or not at all.
- Your requests (time off, shift swaps, training) are denied while others' are approved.
- You're passed over for promotions, training, or better roles.
- Coworkers start distancing themselves after a manager's attitude toward you changes.

# Understanding What's Happening

Unfair treatment usually falls into one of a few categories. Knowing which it might be helps you choose the right response.

## Favoritism

A manager prefers certain employees, often friends, long-time staff, or people who socialize with them. It's frustrating and unfair, but in many places favoritism alone is **not illegal** unless it's connected to something below. It may still violate company policy.

## Discrimination

When you're treated worse **because of** a protected characteristic. Under U.S. federal law, these include race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, and gender identity), national origin, age (40 and over), disability, and genetic information. Many states and cities protect additional characteristics.

Signs it may be discrimination:

- The people treated better tend to share a characteristic you don't.
- Comments or "jokes" about your background, accent, appearance, religion, age, or identity.
- Treatment changed after you shared something personal (a pregnancy, a disability, a religious practice).

## Retaliation

When you're treated worse **because you** did something protected: reported harassment or discrimination, complained about wages or tips, raised a safety concern, took protected leave, or supported a coworker's complaint. Retaliation is illegal in many situations, even if the original complaint isn't proven.

Signs it may be retaliation:

- Treatment changed soon after you complained or reported something.
- Sudden write-ups, schedule cuts, or coldness with no other explanation.

## Personality or communication clash

Sometimes a manager simply doesn't connect with you, or there's a misunderstanding that grew over time. This is still worth addressing, and a direct conversation may help (see Chapter 4).

| Category | Is it illegal? (U.S. generally) | Best first step |
| Favoritism | Usually not, unless tied to a protected characteristic | Document; consider a conversation or going up the chain |
| Discrimination | Yes, under federal and many state laws | Document; report in writing; consider EEOC or an attorney |
| Retaliation | Often, depending on what you reported | Document the timeline; report; consider an attorney |
| Personality clash | No | Direct, calm conversation; ask for clear expectations |

# Self-Check: Is It a Pattern?

Answer honestly. This isn't about proving anything. It's about seeing clearly.

[ ] The different treatment has happened more than a few times
[ ] I can point to specific examples with dates
[ ] Coworkers in similar roles are treated differently in the same situations
[ ] Other people have noticed or commented on it
[ ] The treatment started or got worse after a specific event
[ ] It's affecting my income (shifts, sections, hours)
[ ] It's affecting my health, sleep, or confidence

# Responding Without Making It Worse

When you feel targeted, it's natural to want to push back hard or shut down completely. A calm, strategic approach protects you better.

## Protect your professional record

- **Keep your performance strong.** Be on time, follow the rules, and do your side work. Don't give anyone a legitimate reason to discipline you.
- **Stay professional in writing.** Texts and emails can be shown to others later.
- **Don't vent about management on social media** or in group chats. It can be used against you.
- **Keep records** of your good work too: compliments from guests, sales numbers, positive feedback.

## Consider a direct conversation

If you feel safe doing so, a calm conversation can sometimes change things. Keep it focused on facts and your goals.

| Goal | What you can say |
| Understand scheduling | "I've noticed I've had fewer shifts than usual over the past month. Can you help me understand how shifts are being assigned and what I can do to get more?" |
| Address sections | "I'd love a chance at the main floor on weekends. What would you need to see from me to make that happen?" |
| Address tone | "I've felt some tension between us lately, and I want to fix it. Is there something I've done that we should talk about?" |
| Clarify expectations | "I want to make sure I'm meeting expectations. Could you share any specific feedback on my performance?" |
| Name the pattern | "I've noticed I'm being held to different rules than others on [specific issue]. I'd like to understand why." |

After the conversation, send a short recap by text or email: "Thanks for talking with me today. Just to confirm, you said..."

> Asking for clear, specific expectations is powerful. It either gives you a real path forward or creates a record that the standards are unclear or applied unfairly.

## When a conversation isn't safe or doesn't work

- **Go up the chain:** a general manager, owner, district manager, or HR.
- **Put it in writing.** Guide 03 includes a written-complaint template.
- **Know your outside options:** if you believe you're facing discrimination or retaliation, government agencies like the EEOC or your state's civil rights or labor agency may be able to help, and **deadlines apply.**

# Protecting Your Confidence

Being singled out can slowly wear down how you see yourself. Protecting your self-worth is just as important as protecting your job.

## Separate their behavior from your worth

How a manager treats you says more about the manager, the workplace, or the situation than about your value. You can take feedback seriously without accepting disrespect.

## Build support outside of work

- Talk to friends or family who can remind you who you are outside of work.
- Connect with coworkers you trust, carefully, and without gossip.
- Consider talking to a counselor, especially if you're feeling anxious, depressed, or stuck.

## Remind yourself of the facts

- Keep a **"wins" list:** great guest feedback, big sales nights, skills you've learned.
- Re-read it on hard days.

## Watch for the signs it's getting to you

- Losing sleep or appetite over work
- Dreading every shift
- Feeling hopeless or worthless
- Pulling away from people you care about

If these sound familiar, please reach out for support. See Guide 02 in this series for burnout recovery tools. In the U.S., you can call or text **988** any time if you're in crisis.

# Deciding Your Next Move

You have more options than you may think. There's no single right answer. It depends on your finances, your goals, and how serious the situation is.

| Option | When it may make sense |
| Stay and improve the relationship | It seems like a misunderstanding or clash, and a conversation helped |
| Stay and escalate | The treatment is serious, you want to keep the job, and you're documenting |
| Request a transfer | Your company has other locations or shifts with different management |
| Take outside action | You believe it's discrimination or retaliation and internal steps didn't work |
| Leave for a better workplace | The environment is harming you and you've found (or are building toward) a better option |

## If you decide to leave

- **Start your search while you're still employed,** if possible.
- **Keep copies** of your pay records and documentation before you leave. Only keep your own records, not confidential company information.
- **Give notice professionally** if it's safe to do so, and keep your exit calm.
- **Ask a trusted former coworker or supervisor** to be a reference.
- **Look for red flags and green flags** in your next workplace (see below).

## Green flags in a new restaurant

- Clear, written policies for scheduling, sections, and tip-outs
- Managers who give feedback privately and respectfully
- Low turnover, with staff who have been there for years
- Fair ways to raise concerns, like HR or an owner who listens
